If your coffee tastes sharp, sour, or thin — like unripe fruit rather than ripe fruit — you're tasting under-extraction. That's not a bad bean problem nine times out of ten. It's a brewing problem, and it's fixable in your next cup.

## What sour actually means

Coffee extraction happens in a rough order: acids come out first, then sugars, then bitter compounds. A "balanced" cup has pulled enough sugar and body to round out the acids. A sour cup stopped too early — you got the acids without enough of everything that's supposed to follow.

This is different from coffee that's just naturally bright or fruity. A well-extracted Ethiopian light roast can taste like citrus or berries and still be balanced. Under-extracted sourness feels more like biting into a lemon: puckering, one-note, and a little hollow.

## The three things to check, in order

**1. Grind size.** This is the most common cause and the first thing to adjust. If your grind is too coarse, water flows past the coffee too quickly and doesn't dissolve enough. Go finer, in small steps, and taste again before making another change.

**2. Water temperature.** Water below about 195°F (90°C) struggles to extract efficiently, especially with finer roasts. If your kettle doesn't show temperature, let it sit off the boil for 30 seconds before pouring.

**3. Contact time.** If you're using a method where you control the brew time — pour-over, French press, AeroPress — a too-short brew is often paired with a too-coarse grind. Fix the grind first; time will often follow.

## Change one thing at a time

It's tempting to adjust grind, temperature, and pour speed all in the same brew. Don't. You won't know what fixed it, and you won't be able to repeat the fix tomorrow. Change the grind. Taste. Then, if needed, change the next variable.

Sour coffee is one of the easiest problems in home brewing to solve, because it points to exactly one direction: extract more. Grind finer, brew a little longer, or brew a little hotter — and taste your way there.
