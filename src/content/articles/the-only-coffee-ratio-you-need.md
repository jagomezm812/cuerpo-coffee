Most bad home coffee isn't a bean problem or a technique problem. It's a measuring problem — specifically, not measuring at all. If you're eyeballing scoops, your coffee strength changes every single day, and you have no way to fix it when it's off.

## Start here: 1:16

For every 1 gram of coffee, use 16 grams of water. That's it. That's the ratio that gets you into "good, balanced coffee" territory for almost any brewing method — pour-over, French press, drip machine.

In practical terms:

- **20g coffee : 320g water** — one large mug
- **15g coffee : 240g water** — one standard mug
- **30g coffee : 480g water** — a small pot for two

Weigh both. Grams of water and grams of liquid water are the same thing (1g = 1ml), so if you don't have a scale that measures liquid, weighing your water is still exact — no conversion needed.

## Why grams, not scoops or cups

A "scoop" of coffee can vary in weight depending on grind size, bean density, and how full the scoop actually is. A cup of water measured in a jug is imprecise at the edges, and those edges matter — a 10% difference in either coffee or water is enough to taste as "off" without you knowing why.

A kitchen scale that measures in grams removes all of that variance. It's the single highest-value $15 you can spend on your coffee setup, ahead of any grinder or kettle upgrade.

## Adjust from there, deliberately

1:16 is a starting point, not a law. If a coffee tastes weak or watery to you at 1:16, move toward 1:15. If it tastes heavy or muddy, move toward 1:17. But make that adjustment on purpose, in one direction, and note it — don't drift back to eyeballing.

Once you're weighing coffee and water, every other change you make — grind size, brew time, water temperature — actually means something, because you've removed the biggest variable that was hiding the results.
